# Climate and Flood-Risk Context — Mandi Bahauddin District, Punjab

## Location and Administrative Context

Mandi Bahauddin District lies in the Chaj Doab region of Punjab, Pakistan,
between the Jhelum and Chenab rivers, and spans roughly 2,673 square
kilometres across three tehsils: Mandi Bahauddin, Phalia, and Malakwal
(source: Mandi Bahauddin District Disaster Management Plan, PDMA Punjab,
2020). The district city itself sits at an elevation of approximately
204-219 metres above sea level. Irrigation in the district is drawn mainly
from the Lower Jhelum Canal and partly from the Upper Jhelum Canal.

## Climate Classification

Mandi Bahauddin's climate is classified as BSh (hot semi-arid) under the
Köppen-Geiger system, per a peer-reviewed flood-hazard study on the
district (Ranjha et al., "Evaluating the flood hazard in District Mandi
Bahauddin: a comparative study between 2010 and 2014," ResearchGate,
published around 2018). Summers are very hot, with the district's disaster
management plan citing peak daytime summer temperatures reaching around
45°C; winters are comparatively mild.

Some automated climate-data aggregator sites classify parts of the district
as BWh (subtropical desert) with much lower rainfall figures than the
peer-reviewed and village-level sources below — this is noted as a
discrepancy worth flagging rather than resolved here; the BSh classification
from the peer-reviewed flood study should be treated as the more reliable
figure for AI knowledge-base purposes.

## Rainfall

Average annual rainfall in the district is low and highly seasonal. A
village-level climate record for Chak No. 22 (Tehsil Malakwal, Mandi
Bahauddin District) reports average annual precipitation of about 578 mm,
concentrated heavily in the monsoon season — ranging from roughly 5 mm in
November to roughly 167 mm in August (source: climate-data.org, via
Wikipedia's Chak No. 22 article). This is broadly consistent with a
semi-arid classification and with the ~500 mm figure commonly cited for the
district generally. **Species and irrigation planning should assume most of
the district's annual rainfall arrives in a short monsoon window (roughly
July-September), not evenly across the year.**

## Flood and Waterlogging Risk

Mandi Bahauddin faces significant, recurring flood risk from both the
Jhelum and Chenab rivers:

- The peer-reviewed 2010-2014 comparative flood study found the 2014 flood
  caused more severe socio-economic impact than 2010, with Tehsil Phalia
  particularly affected due to its location along the Chenab river. The
  study's authors recommend a shift in district policy from disaster
  *response* toward disaster *mitigation*.
- The district lies in the Chaj Doab, an area independently identified as
  having high flash-flood susceptibility in a 2024 geospatial assessment
  (Pakistan Journal of Science).
- Recent monsoon flooding (documented in news coverage from August-September
  2025) triggered evacuation warnings around the Qadirabad headworks in
  Mandi Bahauddin due to risk of breach, with flows at nearby Punjab
  headworks reaching extremely high levels; flooding has been severe enough
  in past events to require evacuating the District Jail in Mandi Bahauddin.
- The district's official disaster management plan lists floods, hill
  torrents, and nullahs (seasonal streams) among the mapped hazards, with a
  formal warning/siren protocol in place for high-flood conditions.

**Implication for plantation planning:** low-lying areas near the Jhelum and
Chenab rivers, and areas near canal/drainage infrastructure, should be
treated as having meaningfully elevated waterlogging and flood risk within
a given planting season, not just a rare/historical event — this is
consistent with the flood-tolerant species profile (e.g. Arjun) documented
in the companion tree species evidence base being specifically valuable for
this district.

## Soil

The district's soils are predominantly alluvial, consistent with its
location in the Jhelum-Chenab riverine doab. Urban and peri-urban
expansion is associated with soil compaction and nutrient depletion in
smaller planting spaces, which should be factored into urban plantation
site selection separately from the district's general alluvial soil
character.
