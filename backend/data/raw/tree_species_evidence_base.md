# Tree Species Evidence Base — Candidates for Mandi Bahauddin, Punjab

This document compiles species-level evidence from established agroforestry
references (World Agroforestry Centre's Agroforestree Database, CABI
Compendium, Plants for a Future) and peer-reviewed studies. Values here are
reported ranges from the cited literature, not measurements taken in Mandi
Bahauddin specifically — treat them as general species tolerances to be
cross-checked against local conditions, not site-specific guarantees.

## Shisham (Dalbergia sissoo)

### Botanical Profile
Dalbergia sissoo is a medium to large deciduous nitrogen-fixing tree
reaching up to 30 m in height and 80 cm in diameter under favourable
conditions, native to the Indian subcontinent. It is a leguminous species
(Fabaceae) valued for timber, fuelwood, fodder, and shade.

### Soil and Water Requirements
Reported tolerances span a wide range: annual rainfall of 500-4,500 mm
without irrigation, and soil pH between 5.0 and 8.0, favouring well-drained,
porous soils such as sandy loams, commonly found along riverbanks. Its
aggressive root system makes it useful for erosion control and soil
stabilization on stream and river banks. A comparative drought-stress trial
(Thakur, ISCO 2006, India) found Dalbergia sissoo showed better drought
tolerance than Populus deltoides under withheld irrigation, attributed to
higher water-use efficiency during summer drought.

### Climate Tolerance
Tolerates minimum temperatures down to about -4°C and grows optimally
between 20-45°C, with an optimal (irrigated) rainfall around 2,750 mm/year.

### Known Disease Risk — Shisham Dieback
Shisham dieback is a well-documented, serious threat to cultivated D.
sissoo across Punjab. It is a fungal disease complex primarily associated
with Fusarium solani f. sp. dalbergiae and Botryodiplodia theobromae
(Lasiodiplodia theobromae), sometimes compounded by bacterial wilt and
root-knot nematodes in sandy soils. Symptoms include progressive wilting,
branch cankers, leaf yellowing, and eventual mortality. Drought stress is
reported to amplify susceptibility to these pathogens. This is a significant
caveat for any large-scale Shisham plantation planning in Punjab.

### Plantation Use
Widely used in Punjab for windbreaks, shelterbelts, shade in
intercropping/orchards, urban roadside/garden ornamental planting, and
reforestation programs.

**Sources:** Winrock International, "Sissoo — The Versatile Rosewood" (NFTA
89-07); World Agroforestry Centre Agroforestree Database (Orwa et al. 2009);
CABI Compendium, *Dalbergia sissoo*; Thakur, "Screening of Fuel, Fodder and
Timber Agroforestry Tree Species for Drought Tolerance" (ISCO 2006);
peer-reviewed dieback-disease transcriptome study (PMC10665356).

## Kikar (Acacia nilotica)

### Botanical Profile
Acacia nilotica, known locally as kikar or babul, is a multipurpose
nitrogen-fixing leguminous tree widely used across South Asian agroforestry
systems, valued for fuelwood, fodder, gum, and land reclamation.

### Soil and Water Requirements
Grows under sub-tropical to tropical conditions and withstands drought,
though adequate moisture is needed for full growth. It is specifically
documented as suitable for degraded, nitrogen-deficient, and drought-prone
soils. In India it is used extensively on degraded saline/alkaline soils,
tolerating soil pH up to 9 and soluble salt content below 3%. Field studies
on saline irrigation (Tomar & Minhas) found kikar showed no deterioration in
growth under saline irrigation (ECiw 10.5 dS/m) and performed better than
Shisham under the same conditions. It is also documented among the tree
species used in reclamation of waterlogged and saline soils, alongside
bio-drainage techniques.

### Climate Tolerance
Withstands extreme heat exceeding 50°C.

### Effect on Soil
Reported to improve soil fertility under its canopy over time by increasing
clay content relative to sand, attributed to protecting soil from raindrop
impact.

### Plantation Use
Common windbreak species; used extensively in reclamation of saline,
waterlogged, and degraded land; also intercropped with cereal crops in
traditional Indian agroforestry systems.

**Sources:** "Acacia nilotica: A multipurpose leguminous plant" (Nature and
Science, ResearchGate); Tomar & Minhas et al., saline irrigation study on
*Acacia nilotica* and *Dalbergia sissoo* (calcareous soil); "Acacia nilotica:
A Promising Species for Soil Sustainability"; Jhariya et al., *Land and
Environmental Management through Forestry* (Wiley).

## Neem (Azadirachta indica)

### Botanical Profile
Azadirachta indica is a fast-growing evergreen tree reaching about 15 m in
height and spread, native to South Asia including Pakistan, valued for shade,
timber, and its well-documented insecticidal/medicinal compounds.

### Soil and Water Requirements
Tolerates a wide soil pH range, with sources citing tolerance between pH 4
and 10 (optimal growth around pH 6.2-7.0), and grows in medium (loamy) to
heavy (clay) soils, though it prefers well-drained conditions. It is
notably drought tolerant, documented as surviving on as little as 130 mm of
annual rainfall in some regions, with an optimal range cited around 400-1,200
mm/year. **Waterlogging is a significant limitation** — sources explicitly
note that waterlogged soil conditions can retard growth and increase
mortality risk, since neem depends on a deep taproot system suited to dry
localities rather than saturated soils.

### Climate Tolerance
Optimum growth temperature range is roughly 21-32°C, but it can tolerate up
to 50°C; low temperatures below about 4°C are unfavourable.

### Plantation Use
Widely used as a shade and avenue tree along roads and in urban areas due to
its dense crown and low branching; also used as a windbreak, in
dune-fixation/erosion control due to its deep root system, and intercropped
with field crops such as pearl millet.

**Sources:** Winrock International, "Neem — a versatile tree for the tropics
and subtropics"; Plants for a Future (PFAF) database entry for *Azadirachta
indica*; World Agroforestry Centre species datasheet (AFTPDFS); "Potential
contribution of neem leaves to dry season feeding of ruminants" (LRRD);
Azadirachta Indica literature review compiling Ogemah 2003 and Schmutterer
1995 soil/climate tolerance ranges.

## Arjun (Terminalia arjuna)

### Botanical Profile
Terminalia arjuna is a large deciduous tree (up to 25-30 m) native to the
Indian subcontinent, strongly associated with riparian (riverbank) habitats
and widely used in traditional medicine, agroforestry, and riverbank
stabilization.

### Soil and Water Requirements
This is the standout species for waterlogged/flood-prone conditions among
those reviewed here: multiple sources document good tolerance to soil
salinity, seasonal flooding, and waterlogged soil. It grows on a range of
soil types including alluvial loams, sandy loams, and clay-loam floodplain
soils, and is specifically noted as tolerant of seasonally waterlogged clay
soils. Its buttressed trunk is described as a structural adaptation to soft,
often-waterlogged riparian habitat. Natural regeneration is reported to be
poor in semi-arid habitats due to insufficient moisture — establishment
likely needs supplemental watering in a semi-arid district like Mandi
Bahauddin, even though the mature tree tolerates flooding well.

### Climate Tolerance
Grows best where daytime temperatures range 20-33°C but can tolerate a much
wider range of roughly 5-47°C.

### Ecological Role
A riverine agroforestry study on the Cauvery river system (India) found
Terminalia arjuna to be the single most efficient species for CO2
sequestration among 37 species surveyed, and it is noted for supporting
riparian invertebrate and bird communities.

### Plantation Use
Used for riverbank stabilization, avenue/shade planting (including near
wells, where roots are traditionally believed to help purify water), and
shade in coffee/coconut/citrus intercropping systems.

**Sources:** World Agroforestry Centre Agroforestree Database species
profile (Orwa et al. 2009); IPlantz and PFAF species databases; CABI
Compendium, *Terminalia arjuna*; "Influence of Terminalia arjuna on the
riparian landscapes of the River Cauvery" (ResearchGate); Nema & Khare,
"Effect of waterlogging on some forest plants" (Journal of Tropical
Forestry).

This table is a synthesis of the cited literature above, not an independently

toward Arjun/Kikar over Neem/Shisham), not as a precise ranking.
